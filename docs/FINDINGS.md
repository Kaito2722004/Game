# Tournament Findings

Interpretation of the simulation committed with this project. Every number
here comes from `results/`, generated by:

```bash
python main.py tournament --rounds 100 --save-rounds
```

Settings: 6 strategies, 15 matches (each pair once), 100 rounds per match,
seed 42, no self-play.

## Ranking

| Rank | Strategy | Total | Average/round | Cooperation rate |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | GT — Grim Trigger | 1282 | 2.564 | 0.604 |
| 2 | TFT — Tit-for-Tat | 1219 | 2.438 | 0.700 |
| 3 | TF2T — Tit-for-Two-Tats | 1192 | 2.384 | 0.744 |
| 4 | AD — Always Defect | 1128 | 2.256 | 0.000 |
| 5 | AC — Always Cooperate | 1050 | 2.100 | 0.000* |
| 6 | RAND — Random | 1019 | 2.038 | 0.496 |

\*AC's cooperation rate is 1.000; its *defection* rate is 0.000.

Re-running with `--repeats 200` (3000 matches) leaves the order unchanged, so
the ranking is not an artefact of one lucky seed for RAND.

## Three things the numbers show

**1. The nice strategies fill the top three places.** GT, TFT and TF2T never
defect first, and they take the top three ranks. AD — the strategy that plays
the Nash equilibrium move every round — comes fourth. Individually rational
play does not win a repeated tournament.

**2. Winning the tournament is not the same as beating opponents.** TFT's
match record is 0 wins, 3 draws, 2 losses: it never outscores anyone. It
places second anyway, because it draws 300–300 with every nice strategy and
loses to AD by only 99–104. AD's record is the opposite — 5 wins, 0 losses —
yet it finishes fourth, because winning a match at 1 point per round is worth
less than drawing one at 3 points per round. This is the central lesson of
Axelrod's tournaments and it is worth a full paragraph in the discussion.

**3. Grim Trigger edges ahead of TFT only because of RAND.** The two are
identical against AC, TFT, TF2T and near-identical against AD. The gap comes
from one matchup:

| | vs RAND (payoff per round) |
|---|---:|
| GT | 2.83 |
| TFT | 2.20 |

Against a random opponent, GT defects permanently after the first defection
and then harvests 5 points every time RAND happens to cooperate — it finishes
that match 283–58. TFT keeps forgiving and keeps being exploited half the
time. GT wins this tournament by punishing an opponent that cannot learn.

## Why this does not make Grim Trigger the better strategy

State this honestly rather than declaring GT the winner and stopping:

- **No noise.** There are no mistaken moves in this simulation. Add even a 1%
  chance of a move being flipped and GT's permanent punishment turns a single
  accident into mutual defection for the rest of the match; TFT recovers.
- **The strategy pool decides the winner.** GT's advantage comes entirely from
  RAND being in the pool. Remove RAND and GT and TFT tie.
- **Unforgiveness is fragile, not strong.** GT is nice, retaliatory and clear,
  but it is not forgiving — the one Axelrod property it lacks is exactly the
  one that costs nothing here and would cost a great deal under noise.

Adding a noisy tournament is the strongest single piece of future work for
this project.

## Cooperation over the length of a match

`results/cooperation_over_time.csv` pools all strategies. Cooperation is
highest in the opening block, because every nice strategy opens with C, and
then settles as GT and TFT lock into punishment against AD and RAND. There is
no end-game collapse — unlike human players, none of these strategies knows
the match is about to end, which is itself worth comparing against the
classroom data.
